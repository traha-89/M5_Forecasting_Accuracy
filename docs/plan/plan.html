§1

How the horizons actually line up

This is the single thing most M5 write-ups get muddled, and every later phase depends on getting it right, so it goes first.

sales_train_evaluation.csv carries d_1–d_1941 (2011-01-29 → 2016-05-22). calendar.csv runs 28 days further, to d_1969 (2016-06-19). That extra calendar stretch is not an accident — it exists so the competition's private test period could have features built for it. Which gives us three distinct regions:

RegionDaysDatesActuals?Use
Training d_1 – d_1913 2011-01-29 → 2016-04-24 yes Fit + rolling-origin CV. Everything through P6 lives here.
Holdout d_1914 – d_1941 2016-04-25 → 2016-05-22 yes The honest test set. Scored exactly once, in P7.
Forecast d_1942 – d_1969 2016-05-23 → 2016-06-19 no The final output. Refit on all 1,941 days, predict.

The discipline rule

Because the holdout actuals are sitting right there in the CSV we already have, it is trivially easy to peek — tune against d_1914–d_1941, and the reported score becomes fiction. Model selection, hyperparameters, and ensemble weights are all decided on rolling-origin folds that end at d_1913 or earlier. The holdout is scored once, at the P7 gate, and whatever it says is what we report.

Two consequences worth noting now. First, sales_train_validation.csv is redundant — its d_1–d_1913 columns should be byte-identical to the evaluation file's. P1 asserts that once, then we work only from the evaluation file. Second, sample_submission.csv's 60,980 rows are two stacked blocks of 30,490: the _validation ids want d_1914–d_1941 and the _evaluation ids want d_1942–d_1969. We can fill both, which is a nice free consistency check.

§2

Forecast granularity — one model per item, or not

You asked how to handle the item–store granularity specifically. Here is the recommendation and the reasoning, because this decision drives the whole modeling design.

Not one model per series

Fitting 30,490 separate models is the intuitive read of "item-level forecast" and it is the wrong call here. Each series has at most 1,941 observations, around two-thirds of them zero, and many products were released partway through — so a per-series model is fitting a handful of effective data points. Worse, it cannot learn anything transferable: that Easter suppresses HOBBIES, that a price cut lifts a FOODS item, that SNAP days matter in Texas. Those patterns are shared across items, and a per-series model throws that sharing away. On this box, 30,490 fits is also simply not going to happen.

Global models, partitioned by store

Instead: one model learns across many series at once, with item_id, dept_id, cat_id and store_id as categorical features, so cross-series structure is learned rather than discarded. A single truly global model over all 59.2M rows would strain 16 GB, so we cut it into ten partitions, one per store — about 3,049 series and 5.9M rows each.

That partition is not just a memory convenience, it is the right seam. SNAP schedules are state-specific, price levels and assortment differ by store, and store-level demand regimes are visibly distinct in the M5 data. Ten models fit comfortably, train in parallel-ish batches, and each keeps full cross-item learning within its store. If a store partition still runs hot, the fallback is store × category (30 partitions).

Where per-series does earn its place

The Nixtla StatsForecast arm is per-series — that library is built to fit thousands of small classical models across cores efficiently, and intermittent-demand methods like Croston and TSB are per-series by construction. So the shortlist deliberately contains both shapes, and P7 checks whether they disagree in an exploitable way.

The segmentation question we defer to P7

Rather than guessing now, P2 classifies every series on the ADI / CV² quadrant (smooth, erratic, intermittent, lumpy) and P7 reports each model's error within each quadrant. If the gradient-boosted models win on dense series while Croston-family methods win on the genuinely sparse ones — a common finding — then routing by segment is justified by evidence rather than by folklore. If they don't, we take the single best model and skip the complexity.

P1

Load & hygiene check

Output
01_data_hygiene.ipynb → sales_long.parquet
Effort
1 session
Gate
Assertion suite passes; long-format parquet written & row count recorded

Load with explicit dtypes from the start — day columns as int16, id columns as category. Naive read_csv on the wide file costs several GB in object dtype for no reason; typed, it is around 120 MB.

Structural assertions

  • 30,490 unique ids; id decomposes exactly into item_id + store_id.
  • Evaluation and validation files agree on d_1–d_1913.
  • Calendar has 1,969 contiguous dates, no gaps, and wm_yr_wk maps consistently to date.
  • 10 stores × 3,049 items; 3 states, 3 categories, 7 departments.
  • Submission template has 60,980 rows in two id blocks.

Content checks

  • Negative or implausible sales — expect none; flag loudly if present.
  • Price coverage. A missing sell_prices row means the item was not on sale in that store that week — almost always pre-release. Quantify the leading gap per series; this becomes the release date and lets us drop roughly 12–13% of rows that are structural zeros, not demand signal.
  • Christmas closures. Stores close 25 Dec; every series should read zero on those five dates. Confirm, and mark them so they don't get modeled as demand collapse.
  • Sparsity profile — zero fraction overall and by category, store, department.
  • Dead series — items with no sales in the final 60 days, which need a decision (forecast zero, or let the model decide).
  • Event columns: nulls are meaningful ("no event"), not missing data. Duplicate price rows: expect none.

Then melt to long (id, d, sales), join calendar and prices, drop pre-release rows, and persist as parquet. Full melt is 30,490 × 1,941 = 59,181,090 rows, dropping to roughly 46–47M after the pre-release cut.

P2

Exploratory analysis

Output
02_eda.ipynb + reports/figures/
Effort
1–2 sessions
Gate
A written findings → feature-hypothesis table, each row citing its evidence

EDA here has a job beyond description: every plot should either kill or justify a candidate feature. The gate is that table, not the figure count.

  • Trend and level — total units over time, then split by state, store, category, department. Is growth real or assortment expansion?
  • Seasonality — day-of-week profile (strong and category-dependent), month-of-year, and the annual shape. Weekend lift differs between FOODS and HOBBIES.
  • SNAP. Food-stamp disbursement days should visibly lift FOODS, on a state-specific schedule. Quantify the lift per state — this is one of the highest-value features in the dataset.
  • Events. Effect size per event name and type (Sporting, Cultural, National, Religious), including lead-up and hangover days. Super Bowl, Thanksgiving, Easter, and Christmas behave very differently from one another.
  • Price. Distribution and dispersion; sales response to a price change; how often prices move; whether an item's price relative to its own history predicts better than the raw level.
  • Intermittency. Distribution of zero-fraction across series, and the ADI / CV² quadrant classification that P7 reuses for segment diagnostics.
  • Lifecycle. Release dates over time; series that die mid-panel; the practical shape of "new item, no history".
  • Outliers. Extreme single-day spikes — distinguish genuine promotional demand (keep, it is signal) from implausible values. Given RMSSE squares errors, a handful of spikes can dominate a series' score, so decide the handling policy here and record it.
P3

The metric — before any model

Output
03_metrics.ipynb + src/metrics.py
Effort
1 session
Gate
WRMSSE matches a hand-computed value on a toy panel; unit tests pass

Metric code comes before baselines and models deliberately. A subtly wrong WRMSSE silently invalidates every comparison downstream, and it is far cheaper to get right against a hand-computed toy example than to debug from a suspicious leaderboard later.

WRMSSE, concretely

For each series, RMSSE = sqrt( mean((y − ŷ)²) / mean((yₜ − yₜ₋₁)²) ) — squared forecast error over the horizon, scaled by the in-sample one-step naive error. Two details that are easy to miss and both change the number materially:

  • The denominator is computed only from the series' first non-zero sale onward. Include the leading zeros and the scale is understated, flattering the model.
  • Weights are each series' share of dollar sales over the final 28 training days — units × price, normalized within each aggregation level.

This is then computed across all 12 aggregation levels — total, state, store, category, department, and their crossings, down to item–store — which is 42,840 series in total. WRMSSE is the unweighted mean of the twelve weighted level scores. So a model can be excellent at the item–store level and still score badly if its aggregates are biased, which is worth knowing early.

Supporting metrics

MetricRoleWhy it earns a slot
WRMSSEprimaryThe competition metric. Everything is decided on this.
RMSSE, unweighteddiagnosticBottom level only, equal weight. Separates "good at the big items" from "good everywhere".
MAE / WMAEdiagnosticAbsolute-error view; far less spike-dominated than squared error, so disagreement with RMSSE localizes outlier sensitivity.
MASEdiagnosticSame scaling idea in absolute terms — a widely legible cross-check.
Total bias %guardrailCount models systematically under-forecast. A blunt bias number catches it immediately.
Per-level WRMSSEdiagnosticThe twelve components separately. The most useful debugging output of the whole project.

Deliberately excluded: sMAPE and MAPE. Both divide by actuals, and with roughly 68% zeros they are undefined or explosive across most of this dataset.

Feature engineering

The lag-28 constraint

We forecast 28 days ahead in one shot, so on the last day of the horizon the most recent observed sale is 28 days old. Every lag and rolling window is therefore anchored at lag 28 or longer. That gives a single non-recursive model valid across the whole horizon — no error compounding, no recursive prediction loop, and it is the design that proved most robust in the actual competition.

FamilyFeatures
Calendar wday, month, year, week-of-year, day-of-month, is_weekend, days since panel start
Events event_name_1/2, event_type_1/2 as categoricals; days-to and days-since nearest event
SNAP snap flag matched to the series' own state (not all three columns)
Price sell_price; price / item's rolling mean; price / item's historical max; price change flag; weeks since last price change; price rank within dept×store; count of distinct prices
Lags sales at lag 28, 29, 30, 35, 42, 49, 56, 364
Rolling mean / std / max over 7, 14, 30, 60, 180 windows, all computed on lag-28 sales; same-weekday rolling mean
Intermittency days since last non-zero sale; non-zero count in last 28 / 60; mean sales conditional on non-zero
Identity item_id, dept_id, cat_id, store_id, state_id as native LightGBM categoricals
Encodings mean sales by item, item×store, dept×store, cat×wday — fit on training folds only

Memory discipline

Non-negotiable on 16 GB: sales as int16, all engineered features as float32, ids as category, and build features per store, writing one parquet partition each. Building all ten stores in one frame is the obvious way to run out of memory. At float32 across ~60 features, one store partition is roughly 1.4 GB — comfortable; all ten at once would be around 14 GB, which is not.

P6

Model pilot on CA_1

Output
06_model_pilot.ipynb
Effort
2–3 sessions
Gate

The whole point of piloting: iterate in minutes on 3,049 series, so design errors surface before they cost hours. Everything downstream is a rerun of this notebook at wider scope.

Validation design

Rolling-origin, three folds of 28 days ending d_1857, d_1885, d_1913, each trained only on days before its fold. No random splits, ever — shuffling a time series leaks the future into the past and produces a beautiful, meaningless score.

The four candidates

ModelShapeConfigurationWhy
LightGBM Tweedieglobal / store objective=tweedie, variance_power 1.1, lr 0.03, 128 leaves, early stopping Tweedie handles the zero-inflated, positively-skewed count distribution directly. This family won M5.
LightGBM Poissonglobal / store objective=poisson, otherwise matched Same features, different loss — isolates how much the objective alone is worth, and the two blend well.
XGBoostglobal / store tree_method=hist, count:poisson, depth 8, eta 0.03 Different split algorithm and regularization on identical features — a real check that results aren't a LightGBM artifact.
StatsForecastper series CrostonOptimized, ADIDA, IMAPA, TSB, SeasonalNaive, DynamicOptimizedTheta Purpose-built for intermittent demand, no feature engineering, and the only per-series arm — the contrast is informative on its own.

Hyperparameter work stays light: sane defaults, early stopping, and a small sweep on learning rate and leaf count. On M5, features and validation design move the score far more than tuning does, and an exhaustive search on this hardware is not a good use of the time.

Alternative horizon strategy, if the single-model approach underperforms

The main alternative used by strong M5 solutions is to split the horizon into four 7-day blocks and fit a separate model per block. Days 1–7 can then use lags as short as 7, which is genuinely more information than the lag-28 constraint allows. Cost: 4× the models and 4× the training time. Worth testing in the pilot precisely because it is cheap there — the decision can then be made on evidence before it costs ten stores' worth of compute.

Train each configuration across all ten store partitions and run StatsForecast over the full 30,490 series. Expect roughly 2–4 hours per gradient-boosting configuration on 8 cores, so this phase is scheduled around overnight runs rather than interactive iteration — another reason the design questions get settled in P6.

  • Score every model and baseline on the CV folds; pick the winner and any blend weights there.
  • Then, once: score on the true holdout d_1914–d_1941, full 42,840-series WRMSSE.
  • Break results down per aggregation level, per store, per category, and per ADI/CV² segment.
  • Decide on segment routing — per §2, only if the segment breakdown actually supports it.
  • Check calibration: predicted vs actual totals at each level, and the bias number.

Deliverable is a model card: chosen approach, holdout WRMSSE, the comparison against all five baselines, known weaknesses, and runtime.

§5

Risks worth pricing in now

RiskSeverityMitigation
Out-of-memory during feature build high Per-store partitions, float32/int16 throughout, parquet round-trips between stages. The P5 gate measures peak RAM explicitly rather than hoping.
WRMSSE implemented subtly wrong high P3 precedes all modeling; validated against a hand-computed toy panel and unit tests. The all-zeros baseline is a second independent check.
Leakage via target encodings or rolling stats high Encodings fit on training folds only; all lags ≥ 28; rolling windows anchored at lag 28. Reviewed as an explicit P5 gate item.
Peeking at the holdout high Holdout untouched until the P7 gate. All selection on rolling-origin CV.
Training runtime blows the schedule medium Pilot measures per-store runtime and extrapolates before committing. Fallbacks: reduced history window, store×category partitions, fewer boosting rounds.
Christmas / closure zeros modeled as demand medium Identified in P1, flagged as a feature, handling policy recorded.
New items with no usable history medium Global models handle these via item/dept/category structure — a genuine advantage over per-series fitting. Verified in the P7 segment breakdown.
Logic drift between notebooks medium Metric and feature code live in thin src/ modules the notebooks import (see §3).
